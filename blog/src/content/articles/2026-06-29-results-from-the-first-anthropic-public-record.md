## Background

The AI industry has grown rapidly over the past decade, from narrow applications to increasingly capable systems that can engage in complex reasoning and creative tasks. However, this progress has outpaced public understanding in many cases. While AI researchers and companies debate technical challenges like alignment and safety, the general population has had limited structured opportunities to express their views on these issues.

Previous efforts to measure AI attitudes have been fragmented—limited surveys, social media sentiment analysis, or anecdotal reporting. What was missing was a systematic, nationally representative survey that could provide reliable data on what Americans actually think about AI development, deployment, and governance. This gap matters because public opinion increasingly influences how regulators approach AI policy, how investors evaluate companies, and how talent considers which organizations to join.

Anthropic's creation of the Public Record addresses this gap directly. As a company founded explicitly to develop AI systems that are safe, interpretable, and beneficial, Anthropic has incentive to understand public concerns and values. The survey serves dual purposes: it provides the company with market and public sentiment research, and it contributes to the broader knowledge base about AI attitudes that researchers, policymakers, and other companies can access.

### Key Measurement Areas

The survey likely captures several critical dimensions. First, awareness and understanding: what percentage of Americans have heard of AI, how they define it, and whether they can identify AI systems they use daily. Second, sentiment about risks and benefits: whether people believe AI will create more jobs or eliminate them, whether they trust AI systems in healthcare or criminal justice, and what harms they worry about most.

Third, institutional trust represents a crucial component. Do Americans trust technology companies to develop AI responsibly? What about academic institutions, government agencies, or nonprofit organizations? These questions directly inform public policy discussions about who should govern AI development. Fourth, the survey measures preferences for regulation—what rules or oversight do people believe should govern AI, and how much should government be involved versus industry self-regulation.
