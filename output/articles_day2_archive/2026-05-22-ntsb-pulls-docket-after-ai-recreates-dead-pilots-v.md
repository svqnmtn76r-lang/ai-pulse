# NTSB Removes Public Docket After AI Recreates Voices of Deceased Pilots from Crash Records

## TL;DR

- **The Issue**: AI voice synthesis tools were used to recreate audio of deceased pilots from National Transportation Safety Board investigation documents, raising serious ethical and privacy concerns.
- **The Response**: The NTSB pulled the public docket containing sensitive materials that enabled the voice recreation, marking an unprecedented regulatory response to AI-generated content.
- **The Broader Problem**: This incident highlights gaps in how government agencies handle sensitive investigative data in an era of accessible AI tools, with potential ripple effects across other industries handling confidential records.

## What happened

In a striking collision between aviation safety protocols and emerging AI capabilities, users discovered they could synthesize realistic voice recreations of deceased airline pilots using publicly available National Transportation Safety Board investigation documents. The incident prompted the NTSB to withdraw its public docket—a rare and significant action for the agency responsible for investigating aviation accidents.

According to reporting from Ars Technica, the discovery demonstrated how detailed transcripts and audio materials routinely published as part of crash investigations could be repurposed by AI voice synthesis systems to create convincing audio of individuals who died in the accidents being investigated. The technical feasibility of such recreation raises critical questions about the balance between transparency in safety investigations and protecting the dignity of victims and their families.

The NTSB's decision to remove the docket represents one of the first instances where a federal agency has taken down public investigative materials specifically due to AI misuse concerns. Previously, such documents remained indefinitely accessible as part of the government's commitment to transparency in aviation safety—a principle that has driven improvements across the industry for decades.

The incident occurred amid growing tensions around AI voice synthesis technology, which has become increasingly sophisticated and accessible to non-experts. While legitimate applications include accessibility features and voice acting, the technology's dual-use potential has alarmed privacy advocates and regulatory bodies alike.

## What happens next

The NTSB will likely face pressure to establish new protocols for handling sensitive information in investigations. Industry observers expect other agencies managing similar investigative materials to review their public disclosure policies, potentially creating a chilling effect on transparency. The case will probably influence upcoming Congressional discussions on AI regulation, particularly regarding synthetic media and privacy protections for individuals in public records.

The broader question remains: how can government agencies maintain their commitment to public safety transparency while preventing misuse of sensitive data in the age of AI?
