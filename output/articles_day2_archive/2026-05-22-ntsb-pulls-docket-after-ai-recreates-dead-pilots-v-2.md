- **Point 1**: The NTSB removed a public safety docket after AI voice synthesis tools were used to recreate audio of deceased pilots from official crash investigation documents

## What happened

In a significant pivot on transparency and data access, the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) has withdrawn a public docket following reports that machine learning researchers and hobbyists successfully synthesized realistic voice recreations of deceased pilots using materials from the agency's crash investigation files, according to reporting from Ars Technica.

The incident marks the first documented case where generative AI capabilities have forced a major U.S. safety regulator to retract publicly available investigation data. Cockpit voice recordings and pilot transcripts, traditionally released as part of the NTSB's commitment to public oversight and accident prevention, were leveraged with modern voice cloning technology to generate audio that faithfully reproduced the voices of individuals killed in crashes.

The development has created an immediate tension between two foundational principles: aviation safety's reliance on transparency and investigations' respect for the deceased and their families. The NTSB's decision to pull the docket suggests the agency believes the risks of deepfake recreation and potential misuse of synthetic pilot voices outweigh traditional arguments for public access to safety data.

The move comes as generative AI tools have become increasingly accessible to non-specialists. Voice synthesis platforms now require minimal computational resources and training data, making recreations possible for anyone with basic technical competency and access to recorded samples.

## What happens next

The NTSB is expected to announce revised protocols for releasing investigation materials, likely implementing tiered access systems where sensitive audio materials remain available only to authorized researchers and safety professionals. Other transportation regulators, including the FAA and international aviation authorities, will probably follow suit with comparable restrictions.

The incident raises broader questions about how federal agencies should balance innovation transparency with emerging misuse risks—a challenge that will define regulatory approaches to AI for years to come.
