# Anthropic Releases First Public Record Survey: Understanding AI Attitudes Across America

Anthropic, the AI safety-focused company behind Claude, has launched its inaugural Public Record—a comprehensive national survey designed to measure how Americans perceive artificial intelligence and related technologies. This marks one of the first large-scale efforts by an AI developer to systematically track public sentiment about AI's role in society, offering insights that could shape how both policymakers and companies approach AI governance.

## TL;DR

- **Public Record Initiative**: Anthropic's new survey program tracks American attitudes, beliefs, and concerns about AI across demographics and regions
- **Baseline Measurement**: The first survey establishes a foundational understanding of public opinion that can be compared against future measurements to identify shifts in sentiment
- **Transparency Goal**: By publishing results openly, Anthropic aims to provide objective data that informs public discourse rather than relying on fragmented or industry-biased polling
- **Impact**: This data offers practitioners and policymakers empirical evidence about public concerns, potentially influencing how companies design AI systems and how regulators approach AI policy

## Background

Public opinion research on transformative technologies typically lags behind their rapid development. For emerging technologies like AI, surveys have been fragmented, often commissioned by interested parties with potential biases, or focused narrowly on specific use cases rather than capturing holistic public sentiment.

The AI sector faces a particular credibility challenge. While some polls show Americans hold mixed views on AI—combining optimism about potential benefits with anxiety about job displacement and safety risks—these surveys often come from media outlets, think tanks, or academic institutions without direct involvement from AI companies themselves. This creates an information asymmetry where the public lacks clear, unbiased baseline data about broader attitudes.

Anthropic's decision to conduct and publish its own public record represents an attempt to address this gap. Rather than waiting for external researchers to measure public sentiment, the company is taking direct responsibility for tracking what Americans think about AI. This approach carries both advantages (direct control over methodology) and inherent tensions (questions about independence).

The timing is significant. As AI regulation accelerates globally and domestically, policymakers increasingly need empirical data about constituent concerns. Without it, policy discussions risk being driven by anecdotes or unrepresentative samples rather than systematic evidence.

## How it works

### Survey Design and Methodology

The Public Record functions as a longitudinal tracking study—meaning Anthropic plans to conduct it repeatedly over time to identify trends rather than capturing a single snapshot. The inaugural survey represents the baseline against which future results will be measured.

Like most national surveys, the Public Record samples a representative cross-section of the American population across demographic categories including age, geography, education level, and political affiliation. This approach allows researchers to understand not just aggregate sentiment but also how attitudes diverge across different groups—for instance, whether concerns about job displacement are more prevalent in manufacturing-heavy regions versus technology hubs.

The survey methodology emphasizes scientific rigor. This means using established statistical techniques to weight results appropriately, clearly documenting response rates, and being transparent about potential sources of bias or uncertainty. Given that Anthropic is itself interested in the results, the company has an incentive to follow best practices to maintain credibility—publishing results that appear cherry-picked or methodologically questionable would undermine the initiative's value.

### What the Survey Measures

The Public Record captures multiple dimensions of AI attitudes rather than reducing opinion to a single metric. This includes questions about perceived benefits (economic growth, scientific advancement, medical breakthroughs), risks (job displacement, misuse for surveillance, autonomous weapons), and trust in various institutions to manage AI development responsibly.

Crucially, the survey distinguishes between abstract attitudes about "AI" and opinions about specific applications. Americans might simultaneously express concern about general AI development while viewing AI in healthcare positively. These nuances matter for policymakers designing regulation that matches actual public concern rather than imposing blanket restrictions based on hypothetical fears.

The survey also measures what might be called "AI literacy"—the extent to which respondents understand what AI is, how it works, and where it's already deployed in their lives. This data reveals where public knowledge gaps exist, suggesting areas where communication or education from companies and institutions might be most valuable.

### Publication and Transparency

What distinguishes this effort from proprietary market research is Anthropic's commitment to publishing results openly. Raw data, methodology documentation, and detailed breakdowns by demographic group become available to researchers, journalists, and policymakers rather than remaining internal proprietary information.

This transparency approach serves multiple constituencies. Academic researchers can validate findings or conduct secondary analyses. Journalists can fact-check claims about public sentiment that companies might make in policy discussions. Competing AI companies can access the same baseline data rather than each conducting expensive proprietary surveys. And the public gains access to information about their own collective views.

## Why this matters

The Public Record addresses a genuine gap in public understanding of public opinion. Major regulatory moments—like the EU's AI Act negotiations or U.S. Congressional hearings on AI—often proceed with limited empirical data about what constituents actually want. Companies might claim their approach reflects public values, but without systematic measurement, these claims remain assertions.

Additionally, the longitudinal design matters. A single survey captures one moment; tracking the same questions over months and years reveals whether public concern about AI is rising or falling, whether specific events shift sentiment, and whether communication efforts actually move opinion. This baseline will become increasingly valuable as AI capabilities advance and real-world impacts become more apparent.

For practitioners building AI systems, the survey provides insight into which concerns are most salient across different communities. A company deploying AI in hiring, for instance, can see whether their target market shares broader public skepticism about AI in employment decisions and adjust accordingly.

## What happens next

Anthropic has committed to repeating the Public Record periodically, creating a historical record of how American attitudes toward AI evolve. The next surveys will reveal whether sentiment shifts in response to AI breakthroughs, negative incidents, regulatory changes, or other events.

The initiative also invites scrutiny. Researchers and critics will examine methodology, question whether particular framings bias results in Anthropic's favor, and compare findings to other surveys measuring similar constructs. This peer review process is healthy—it strengthens the credibility of findings that withstand scrutiny.

For those following AI policy and development, the Public Record becomes a reference point. When claims about "what the public wants" emerge in policy discussions, stakeholders can point to systematic evidence rather than anecdotes or speculation.
