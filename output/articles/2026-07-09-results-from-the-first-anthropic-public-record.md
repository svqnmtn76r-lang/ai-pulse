# Anthropic Releases First Public Record on AI Attitudes: What Americans Really Think About AI

Anthropic, the AI safety company behind Claude, has published initial findings from its new Anthropic Public Record—a comprehensive national survey designed to track public opinion on artificial intelligence. The initiative marks an attempt to create ongoing, systematic data about how Americans view AI technology, its risks, and its potential benefits.

This matters because AI policy, regulation, and corporate practices increasingly depend on understanding public sentiment. As AI systems become more integrated into daily life, having reliable data on what people actually think—beyond anecdotal headlines—becomes crucial for policymakers, technologists, and the broader public discourse.

- **Public sentiment baseline**: The survey provides the first systematic measurement of how diverse Americans view AI development, safety concerns, and beneficial applications
- **Tracking mechanism**: Anthropic Public Record functions as an ongoing measurement tool, allowing comparisons over time as AI technology and public awareness evolve
- **Impact for practitioners**: Companies developing AI systems, policymakers drafting regulations, and researchers studying AI adoption now have empirical data to inform decisions rather than relying on assumption or anecdotal evidence

## Background

The development of large language models and generative AI has created a sharp disconnect between technical progress and public understanding. While AI capabilities have advanced rapidly over the past few years, public awareness of these systems remains uneven. Some people express enthusiasm about AI's potential in healthcare, education, and productivity. Others worry about job displacement, misinformation, privacy, and existential risks.

Previous attempts to measure AI sentiment have been limited in scope—often conducted by individual companies with potential conflicts of interest, or by academic researchers studying narrow populations. A more systematic, transparent approach to national surveying could provide neutral ground for understanding where Americans actually stand on AI issues.

Anthropic's decision to publish this survey publicly reflects a broader trend among AI companies toward transparency and accountability. Rather than keeping such insights proprietary, releasing research as a "public record" signals an intention to contribute to informed public discourse.

## How it works

### Survey Design and Methodology

The Anthropic Public Record operates as a national survey instrument administered to a representative sample of American adults. The survey measures attitudes across multiple dimensions: perceived risks and benefits of AI, trust in different institutions to develop AI responsibly, support for various regulatory approaches, and awareness of AI systems currently in use.

By conducting this as a public record—meaning the methodology, sample characteristics, and raw findings are publicly documented—Anthropic enables independent verification and reproduction. This transparency reduces bias that might occur if results were presented selectively or interpreted through a single institutional lens.

The survey tracks both broad sentiment and specific concerns. Rather than asking simply "do you like AI?", it measures nuanced positions on specific applications, regulatory preferences, and demographic variations in opinion. This granularity reveals that public opinion isn't monolithic; support varies significantly based on AI use case, demographic factors, and prior awareness of AI systems.

### Tracking Longitudinal Change

The Public Record framework is explicitly designed as an ongoing measurement tool rather than a one-time snapshot. By administering identical or comparable surveys over time, Anthropic aims to track how public opinion evolves as AI technology develops, as people gain more personal experience with AI systems, and as media coverage and policy discussions shift.

This longitudinal approach serves multiple purposes. It can reveal whether initial anxiety about AI diminishes as systems become more familiar, whether specific incidents or regulatory changes move public opinion, and whether demographic groups that initially expressed skepticism become more favorable over time. Conversely, it might show deepening concerns about particular risks as people learn more about capability or deployment decisions.

For researchers, policymakers, and company leaders, having comparable data points across time eliminates guesswork about whether sentiment is actually changing or whether headlines simply create the appearance of change.

### Demographic and Segmentation Analysis

The survey captures responses across demographic categories—age, education level, geography, political affiliation, and others—revealing whether AI attitudes are distributed evenly or clustered in certain populations. Someone's age, education background, or prior tech experience often predicts their views on AI more reliably than general statements about "public opinion."

These segments matter because they indicate where communication, education, or policy interventions might be most needed. If particular groups express high skepticism about AI, understanding the source of that skepticism—whether it's distrust of institutions, concern about job displacement, or simply unfamiliarity—helps shape more targeted responses.

## Why This Approach Matters

Traditional public opinion polling on technology often appears in academic journals or in reports from companies with financial interests in particular outcomes. The Public Record model attempts to establish something closer to a neutral measurement infrastructure—similar to how economists rely on government employment data or inflation indices rather than corporate-produced statistics.

For AI specifically, this infrastructure becomes increasingly important as the technology touches more aspects of society. Regulators in various countries are drafting AI legislation based partly on assumptions about public concern. Companies are making decisions about where to deploy systems based partly on perceived acceptability. Researchers are designing safety measures partly in response to perceived risks. Having empirical data about what people actually think and worry about, rather than what industry observers assume they think, should improve all these decisions.

The public record approach also signals that Anthropic views public opinion as legitimate data worth collecting and sharing transparently, rather than as a variable to be managed or shaped through marketing.

## What happens next

The initial results establish a baseline. Future iterations of the Anthropic Public Record will enable tracking whether sentiment shifts as AI capabilities advance, as policy frameworks develop, and as ordinary people have more direct experience with these systems. The survey results themselves become a resource for other researchers, journalists, and policymakers who want to understand American attitudes toward AI with some empirical rigor.

For organizations working on AI deployment, policy, or safety, these findings provide an important reality check on assumptions about what concerns the public most and where potential friction points might emerge.
