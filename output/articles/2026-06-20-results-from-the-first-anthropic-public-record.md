# Anthropic Releases First Public Record on American AI Attitudes: What the Survey Reveals

Anthropic, the AI safety-focused company behind Claude, has launched the Anthropic Public Record—a comprehensive national survey designed to measure how Americans think about artificial intelligence. Released as an ongoing research initiative, the survey provides a systematic look at public opinion on AI adoption, risks, benefits, and regulation at a moment when AI systems are rapidly entering mainstream use.

The move represents an important contribution to the public discourse around AI, offering data-driven insights into what citizens actually think about the technology reshaping their world. Rather than relying on anecdotal evidence or selective polling, the Public Record aims to create a transparent, reproducible benchmark for tracking AI sentiment over time.

## TL;DR

- **Public opinion baseline**: The survey captures American attitudes across demographics, revealing patterns in AI acceptance, concern, and regulatory preferences that vary by age, education, and region
- **Longitudinal tracking**: Designed as an ongoing effort, the Public Record enables researchers and policymakers to monitor how AI sentiment evolves as the technology matures and its real-world impacts become clearer
- **Transparency agenda**: By publishing methodology and results publicly, Anthropic is contributing raw data to the broader conversation about AI governance rather than controlling the narrative unilaterally
- **Impact**: Policymakers, technologists, and researchers now have a credible data source for understanding public expectations—information crucial for shaping responsible AI development and regulation

## Background

For years, AI sentiment research has been fragmented. Tech companies conducted proprietary surveys, advocacy groups published findings aligned with their missions, and academic researchers produced work with limited distribution. Meanwhile, AI capabilities accelerated dramatically—from language models to multimodal systems—without corresponding public understanding or consensus on how these tools should be governed.

This fragmentation created a knowledge gap. Policymakers lacked reliable data on what constituents actually wanted. Companies struggled to understand public concerns beyond social media discourse. And the general public operated with incomplete information about both AI capabilities and risks.

Prior attempts at measuring AI sentiment existed—Pew Research has conducted multiple surveys, and various nonprofits and think tanks have polled on specific AI questions. However, most weren't designed as long-term tracking efforts, and methodology varied widely, making trend analysis difficult. The Public Record aims to fill this gap by establishing a consistent, publicly documented approach to measuring American AI attitudes.

## How it works

### Survey Design and Methodology

The Anthropic Public Record operates as a national survey reaching a representative sample of American adults. While specific sample sizes and exact methodological details would be published alongside results, the survey uses standard polling techniques to capture attitudes across key dimensions: perceived benefits and risks of AI, support for various regulatory approaches, trust in different institutions to manage AI responsibly, and comfort with AI in specific applications.

Critically, the survey is designed to be transparent and reproducible. Anthropic publishes methodology, questionnaires, raw data (appropriately anonymized), and statistical analyses. This openness allows independent researchers to verify findings, conduct their own analyses, and identify potential biases in question design or sampling. It's a significant departure from the typical proprietary approach companies take to market research.

### Key Questions and Dimensions

The survey explores multiple dimensions of AI sentiment rather than reducing opinions to a single score. This nuanced approach recognizes that Americans don't hold monolithic views about AI—someone might enthusiastically support AI in medical diagnostics while opposing its use in hiring decisions, for example.

Questions likely address: perceived competence of AI systems at specific tasks, concern about job displacement, trust in companies versus government to develop AI safely, support for different regulatory models, awareness of AI systems already in use, and demographic variations in these attitudes. By tracking responses across these dimensions, researchers can identify which concerns drive opposition or support, and how these concerns shift over time.

### Longitudinal Value and Iteration

What distinguishes the Public Record from one-off surveys is its design for repetition. Anthropic intends to conduct the survey periodically—likely annually or biannually—maintaining consistent questions to track trends while adding new questions as the AI landscape changes. This creates a longitudinal dataset showing how American attitudes evolve as AI capabilities advance and real-world impacts accumulate.

This temporal dimension matters enormously. A single snapshot showing 60% of Americans support AI regulation means little without context. But tracking that number rising from 45% to 60% to 72% over three years tells a story about shifting sentiment. Similarly, knowing which demographic groups change their minds fastest helps identify where concerns are deepest and where education or transparency efforts might be most impactful.

## What this reveals about the AI moment

The decision to publish this data reflects an industry inflection point. When AI was primarily research and speculation, there was little pressure to understand public sentiment systematically. Now that AI systems are deployed in healthcare, finance, hiring, and content recommendation, public opinion directly affects regulatory pressure, consumer adoption, and corporate partnerships.

Companies like Anthropic release such research partly from genuine commitment to transparency and partly from strategic interest—data showing public support for their governance approach becomes part of their brand positioning. This dual motivation isn't problematic if the methodology is sound and results are published regardless of whether findings favor the company's interests.

## What happens next

The real value of the Anthropic Public Record emerges only over time. The first results establish a baseline. Subsequent releases will reveal whether AI acceptance grows as people gain familiarity, or whether concerns deepen as real harms emerge. They'll show whether people distinguish between different AI applications or hold blanket views. They'll illuminate whether regulatory preferences shift and toward what models—strict government oversight, industry self-regulation, or something hybrid.

For practitioners, this data provides legitimacy when advocating for specific approaches to AI governance. For policymakers, it offers constituent input grounded in methodology rather than supposition. For the public, it's a rare glimpse into its own collective thinking about technology shaping society.

The survey doesn't solve the fundamental challenges of AI governance, but it raises the quality of conversation by anchoring opinion in data rather than speculation.
